RKHS of the stochastic wave equation

RKHS of the harmonic oscillator

We begin by studying the Fourier modes of the stochastic wave equation, as this will provide insight into the overall structure of the RKHS associated with the stochastic wave equation. The solution of the parametric stochastic wave equation on the bounded domain $\Lambda=(0,\pi)$ with zero initial conditions, driven by space-time white noise, can be written as a Fourier series $$ \begin{equation} \tag{1} X(t)(x)= \sqrt{\frac{2}{\pi}} \sum_{k=1}^\infty \begin{pmatrix} u_k(t)\\v_k(t) \end{pmatrix}\sin(kx),\quad x \in (0,\pi), \quad t \in [0,T]. \end{equation} $$ In view of [1] the resulting Fourier modes in (1) follow a bivariate Ornstein-Uhlenbeck dynamic also called harmonic oscillator $$ \begin{equation} \tag{2} \begin{pmatrix} \mathrm{d} u_k(t)\\ \mathrm{d}v_k(t) \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ -\vartheta_0 k^{2} & 0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} u_k(t)\\v_k(t) \end{pmatrix} + \begin{pmatrix} 0\\ 1 \end{pmatrix}\mathrm{d}w_k(t), \quad u_k(0)=v_k(0)=0, \quad \quad k \in \mathbb{N}. \end{equation} $$ Any weak solution to an infinite SDE in the sense of [2] also induces a mild solution, leading to the following representation for the harmonic oscillator (2): $$ \begin{align} \tag{3} u_k(t)= \frac{1}{\sqrt{\vartheta_0}k}\int_{0}^{t} \sin(\sqrt{\vartheta_0}k (t-s))\mathrm{d}w_k(s), \quad v_k(t)= \int_{0}^{t}\cos(\sqrt{\vartheta_0}k(t-s))\mathrm{d}w_k(s). \end{align} $$ Consider the first component $(u_k(t), t \in [0,T])$ only. The covariance operator of this process is given by $$ \begin{equation} \tag{4} \begin{aligned} &C_{u_k, \vartheta}[f](t) \\ &= \int_{0}^{T}\mathbb{E}[u_k(t)u_k(s)]f(s)\mathrm{d}s\\ &=\int_{0}^{T} \frac{{\color{purple} 2 (t \land s) k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}\cos(k(t-s)\sqrt{\vartheta_0})} + \sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}|t-s|){\color{black}- \sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t+s))}}{4k^3 \vartheta_0^{3/2}} f(s)\mathrm{d}s. \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ for $f \in L^{2}([0,T])$ and $t \in [0,T]$. Consider the mapping $$ \begin{equation} \tag{5} \label{eq:symbolic_kernel} \begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}_{A}(t,s) &:= \frac{ 2 (t \land s)A\cos((t-s)A) + \sin(|t-s|A)- \sin((t+s)A)}{4 A}\\ &= \frac{1}{2}(t \land s) \cos((t-s)A) + \frac{A^{-1}}{4}\Big(\sin(|t-s|A)-\sin((t+s)A)\Big). \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ The first derivative of (5) is given by $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:first_derivative_symbolic_kernel_non_simplified} \tag{6} \begin{aligned} \partial_{s}\mathcal{K}_{A}(t,s) &= \frac{1}{2}(t \land s)A \sin((t-s)A) + \frac{1}{2}H(t-s)\cos((t-s)A) \\ &+ \frac{1}{4}\cos(|t-s|A)\mathrm{sign}(s-t)-\frac{1}{4}\cos((t+s)A), \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ with the Heaviside function $H$ satisfying $$ \begin{equation} \tag{7} \label{eq:heaviside_sign} 2H(t-s)-\mathrm{sign(t-s)}\equiv 1, \quad t,s \in [0,T]. \end{equation} $$ Using (7) and the symmetry of the cosine, we can simplify (6) in order to obtain $$ \begin{equation} \tag{8} \label{eq:first_derivative_symbolic_kernel_simplified} \mathcal{K}_{A}'(t,s) = \frac{1}{2}(t \land s)A \sin((t-s)A) + \frac{1}{4}\cos((t-s)A)-\frac{1}{4}\cos((t+s)A). \end{equation} $$ We may now compute the derivatives of (8), which are given by $$ \begin{align} & \begin{aligned} \label{eq:derivative_2} \mathcal{K}_{A}''(t,s) &= \frac{1}{2}H(t-s)A\sin((t-s)A)- \frac{1}{2}(t \land s) A^{2}\cos((t-s)A) \\ &+ \frac{A}{4}[\sin((t-s)A) + \sin((t+s)A)] \end{aligned}\\ & \begin{aligned} \label{eq:derivative_3} \mathcal{K}_{A}'''(t,s) &=- \frac{1}{2}A^{3}(t \land s) \sin(A(t-s))- A^{2}\cos(A(t-s))H(t-s)-\frac{1}{2}A \sin(A(t-s))\delta(t-s) \\ &+ \frac{1}{4}[-A^{2}\cos(A(t-s)) + A^{2} \cos(A(t+s))] \end{aligned}\\ & \begin{aligned} \mathcal{K}_A''''(t,s) &= \frac{1}{2}A^{4}(t \land s)\cos(A(t-s)) - \frac{3}{2}A^{3}\sin(A(t-s))H(t-s)\\ &+\frac{3}{2}A^{2}\cos(A(t-s))\delta(t-s) + \frac{1}{2}A \sin(A(t-s))\delta^{(1)}(t-s)\\&+ \frac{1}{4}[-A^{3}\sin(A(t-s))-A^{3}\sin((t+s)A)]. \end{aligned} \end{align} $$
Theorem 1. (RKHS of the stochastic harmonic oscillator)

The reproducing kernel Hilbert space associated with the process $(u_k(t), t \in [0,T])$ defined in (3) is given by \begin{equation} \label{eq:rkhs_u_k} H=H_{u_k} = \{h \in L^{2}([0,T])\colon h, h' \in AC([0,T]), h', h'' \in L^{2}([0,T]), h (0)=h'(0)=0\}, \end{equation} with respect to the inner product \begin{align} \tag{9} \label{eq:inner_product_H_u_K} \langle h_1, h_2\rangle_{H_{u_k}}&:=\int_{0}^{T}(\vartheta_0k^2 h_1(t)+h_1''(t))(\vartheta_0k^2h_2(t)+h''_2(t))\mathrm{d}t, \quad h_1, h_2 \in H. \end{align}

Proof.

The covariance function of the process $(u_k(t), t \in [0,T])$ is given by $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:kernel} \begin{aligned} K_{u_k}(t,s)&:=\mathbb{E}[u_k(t)u_k(s)] = \frac{1}{\vartheta_0 k^2}\int_{0}^{t\land s}\sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-r))\sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(s-r))\mathrm{d}r\\ &= \frac{{\color{purple} 2 (t \land s) k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}\cos(k(t-s)\sqrt{\vartheta_0})} + \sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}|t-s|){\color{black}- \sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t+s))}}{4k^3 \vartheta_0^{3/2}}\\ &= \frac{1}{\vartheta_0 k^{2}}\mathcal{K}_{k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}}(t,s), \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ where $\mathcal{K}$ is defined through (5). We need to verify that the mapping $K_{u_k}^{t}(s):= K_{u_k}(t,s)$ satisfies $K_{u_k}^{t} \in H_{u_k}$. This is clear, because since all the cosine and sine functions are continuous and bounded we have $K_{u_k}^{t} \in L^{2}([0,T])$ and clearly $K_{u_k}^{t}(0)=K_{u_k}(t,0)=0$. In view of the derivatives of (5) the derivatives of $K_{u_k}^{t}$ are given by $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:first_deriv_K_u_k} \begin{aligned} (K_{u_k}^{t})'(s)&= \frac{1}{4k^{3}\vartheta_0^{3/2}}\Big( k\sqrt{\vartheta_0} \big[\cos(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))-\cos(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0} (t+s)) \big] \\ &+2 \vartheta_0 k^2 (t \land s) \sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\Big)\\ (K_{u_k}^{t})''(s)&= \frac{1}{4k^{3}\vartheta_0^{3/2}}\Big(k^2 \vartheta_0 \big[{\color{black}\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t+s))} + { \sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))}\big]\\ &+ 2\vartheta_0 k^2 \sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))H(t-s)- {\color{purple}2 \vartheta_0^{3/2}k^3 (t \land s)\cos(\sqrt{\vartheta_0}k(t-s)) }\Big). \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ We notice that all functions with distribution valued derivatives have cancelled out and $(K_{u_k}^{t})' \in L^{2}([0,T])$ with $(K_{u_k}^{t})'(0)=0$. The second derivative is also an $L^{2}([0,T])$-function. Similar to (7) we use the fact that $1 +2 H(t-s)+\mathrm{sign}(t-s)=4H(t-s)$ and we observe $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:nice_sum} \begin{aligned} \vartheta_0 k^{2}K_{u_k}^{t}(s)+ (K_{u_k}^{t})''(s)&= \frac{1}{4k^{3}\vartheta_0^{3/2}}\big(4k^2 \vartheta_0H(t-s) \sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\big)\\ &= \frac{1}{k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}}H(t-s)\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s)). \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ This expression allows us to check the reproducing property of the kernel and we compute $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:plugin_reproducing_property} \begin{aligned} &\langle h, K_{u_k}^{t}(\cdot)\rangle_{H_{u_k}}\\ &= \int_{0}^{T}(\vartheta_0k^{2}h (s)+h''(s))\frac{1}{k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}}H(t-s)\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\mathrm{d}s\\ &= \sqrt{\vartheta_0}k\int_{0}^{t} h (s)\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\mathrm{d}s + \frac{1}{k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}}\int_{0}^{t}h''(s)\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\mathrm{d}s. \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ By partially integrating twice, we obtain for the second summand in the latter expression $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:partial_integration} \begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{t}h''(s)\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\mathrm{d}s\\ &= [ h'(s)\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))]^{t}_0 + k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}\int_{0}^{t}h'(s)\cos(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\mathrm{d}s\\ &=[h'(s)\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))]^{t}_0 + k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}[h (s) \cos(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))]_0^{t} \\ &- k^{2}\vartheta_0 \int_{0}^{t}h (s)\sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))\mathrm{d}s \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ Resubstituting back yields $$ \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} \langle h, K_{u_k}^{t}(\cdot)\rangle_{H_{u_k}} &= \frac{1}{k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}} [h'(s)\sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t-s))]_0^{t} + [h (s) \cos(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0} (t-s))]_0^{t}\\ &=h(t)-\frac{1}{k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}}h'(0)\sin(k\sqrt{\vartheta_0}(t))- h (0)\cos(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}t)\\ &= h (t), \end{aligned} \end{equation} $$ where we have used that $h'(0)= h(0)=0$ since $h \in H_{u_k}$.

The mapping (9) is bilinear \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} &\langle \alpha h_0 + h_1, h_2\rangle_{H_{u_k}}\\&=\int_{0}^{T}(\vartheta_0k^{2}(\alpha h_0(t) + h_1(t)) + \alpha h_0''(t) + h_1''(t))(\vartheta k^{2}h_2(t)+ h''_2(t))\mathrm{d}t\\ &= \int_{0}^{T} (\alpha(\vartheta_0k^{2}h_0(t)+h_0''(t))+ \vartheta_0 k^{2}h_1(t)+h_1''(t))(\vartheta k^{2}h_2(t) + h''_2(t))\mathrm{d}t\\ &= \alpha \langle h_0, h_2\rangle_{H_{u_k}} + \langle h_1, h_2\rangle_{H_{u_k}}, \end{aligned} \end{equation} and also symmetric by the symmetry of the $L^{2}([0,T])$-inner product, as \begin{equation} \langle h_1, h_2\rangle_{H_{u_k}}=\langle \vartheta_0 k^{2}h_1+h_1'', \vartheta_0 k^{2}h_2+h_2''\rangle_{L^{2}([0,T])}. \end{equation} Suppose that $\vartheta_0k^{2}h + h''$ for $h \in H_{u_k}$ is not identical to zero. Then, by the positive-definiteness of the $L^{2}([0,T])$-inner product we also have $\langle h, h\rangle_{H_{u_k}}>0$. Suppose now that $h \in H_{u_k}$ satisfies \begin{equation} \vartheta_0k^{2}h + h'' \equiv 0. \end{equation} This expression is the undamped linear harmonic oscillator, which is solved by functions of the form \begin{equation} \label{eq:base_functions} \begin{aligned} h (t)= a_1\cos(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}t) + b_1\sin(k \sqrt{\vartheta_0}t). \end{aligned} \end{equation} for arbitrary constants $a_1, b_1 \in \mathbb{R}$. Clearly, we have $h (0)= a_1$ and $h'(0)=bk \sqrt{\vartheta_0}$. Thus, the only (such) function of this form in $H_{u_k}$ is identical to zero. The positive-definiteness now follows from the positive-definiteness of the $L^{2}([0,T])$-inner product.

We have shown that $(H_{u_k}, \langle \cdot, \cdot\rangle_{H_{u_k}})$ is a reproducing kernel space. It is also clear that $C_c^{\infty}((0,T)) \subset H_{u_k}$. Therefore, as $C_c^{\infty}((0,T))$ is a dense subset of $L^{2}([0,T])$ with respect to the $L^{2}$-norm we can conclude that $H^{u_k}$ is dense in $L^{2}([0,T])$ as well. Let $C_{u_k}$ be the covariance operator associated with $(u_k(t), t \in [0,T])$ on $L^{2}([0,T])$. Then, as computed in the first step, we have $\Vert h \Vert_{H_{u_k}} = \Vert C_{u_k}^{-1/2}h \Vert_{L^{2}([0,T])}$ for $h \in H_{u_k}=\mathcal{R}(C_{u_k}^{1/2})$. Thus, we know that $\mathcal{R}(C_{u_k}^{1/2})$ is a dense subset of $L^{2}([0,T])$. This in turn implies that $\mathrm{ker}(C_{u_k}^{1/2})=\{0\}$. The completeness follows immediately using Proposition C.0.3 in [3].

∎

RKHS of the stochastic wave equation

The first component of the stochastic wave equation can also be written as $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:stochastic_convolution} u(t)= \int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)\mathrm{d}W(s)= \int_{0}^{t} (-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2}\sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})\mathrm{d}W(s) , \quad t \in [0,T], \end{equation} $$ using the representation \begin{equation} \label{eq:operator_sine_to_sine_operator} S_{\vartheta}(t)= (-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2}\sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2}), \quad t \in [0,T]. \end{equation}
Theorem 1. (RKHS associated with $u$)

Let $(H_{u}, \langle \cdot, \cdot\rangle_{H_{u}})$ be the reproducing kernel Hilbert space associated with $(u(t), t \in [0,T])$. It is given by the set of functions \begin{equation} \label{eq:base_space_RKHS_SPDE} \begin{aligned} H_{u}= \Big\{h \in L^{2}([0,T], D(\Delta)) \colon h, h' \in &\mathrm{AC}([0,T], L^{2}(\Lambda)), \\ \Delta h, h', h'' \in &L^{2}([0,T], L^{2}(\Lambda)), h (0)=0, h'(0)=0\Big\} \end{aligned} \end{equation} with the inner product \begin{equation} \label{eq:inner_product_infinte_dim} \langle h, g\rangle_{H_u} := \int_{0}^{T}\langle (-\vartheta \Delta h) (t) + h''(t), (-\vartheta \Delta g)(t) + g''(t)\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)} \mathrm{d}t. \end{equation}

Proof.

The proof for the stochastic wave equation is similar to the case of the harmonic oscillator. Using Proposition 4.28 in [2], we can compute the correlation operator associated with the process $(u(t), t \in [0,T])$ which is given by \begin{equation} \label{eq:correlation_operator_u} \begin{aligned} K_{u}(t,s) &:=\int_{0}^{t \land s} S_{\vartheta}(t-r) S_{\vartheta}(s-r) \mathrm{d}r \\ &= (-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1}\int_{0}^{t \land s} \sin((t-r)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2}) \sin((s-r)(-\vartheta_0 \Delta)^{1/2}) \mathrm{d}r, \end{aligned} \end{equation} where we have used that $(-\vartheta \Delta)$ and its fractional powers commute with all trigonometric operator families associated with $(-\vartheta \Delta)$, see for example Proposition B.7 and Proposition 3.15 in [4]. In view of Proposition 3.14.5 (f) in [4], the product-to-sum formula still holds for operator cosine and sine functions, yielding: \begin{equation} \label{eq:K_u} \begin{aligned} K_{u}(t,s) &= \frac{(-\vartheta \Delta)^{-3/2}}{4}\Big( \sin(|t-s|(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})- \sin((t+s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2}) \\ &+ 2 (t \land s) (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2} \cos((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})\Big)\\ &= \frac{(-\vartheta \Delta)^{-3/2}}{4}\Big( \sin(|t-s|(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})- \sin((t+s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})\Big)\\ &+ (t \land s) \frac{(-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1}}{2} \cos((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})\\ &= (-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1}\mathcal{K}_{(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2}}(t,s). \end{aligned} \end{equation} Now, we would like to verify that for some function $q \in D(\Delta)$, we have $K_{u}(t, \cdot)q \in H_{u}$. As $q \in D(\Delta)$, Theorem 3.4.11 in [4] or Section 2 in [5] reveal that the mappings $\sin(\cdot (-\vartheta\Delta)^{1/2})q$, $\cos(\cdot (-\vartheta\Delta)^{1/2})q$ are differentiable in time and satisfy \begin{equation} \label{eq:derivative_properties} \begin{aligned} \partial_t \sin(\cdot (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q &= (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2}\cos(\cdot (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q,\\ \partial_t \cos(\cdot (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q &= (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2}\sin(\cdot (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q,\\ \partial_t S_{\vartheta}(t)q &= C_{\vartheta}(t)q = \cos(\cdot (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q \end{aligned} \end{equation} It is clear that the Laplace operator $-\vartheta\Delta$ commutes with $\partial_t$ by the equality of mixed partial derivatives. Since $\sin(t (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q \in D(\Delta)$ and $\cos(t (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q \in D(\Delta)$ for any $t \in [0,T]$, we also observe that $\Delta^{-1}$ commutes with $\partial_t$. Thus, applying symbolic derivations from the beginning yields: \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} (K_{u}(t, \cdot)q)'(s) &= \Big( \frac{(-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1}}{4} \big[\cos((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})-\cos( (t+s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2}) \big]q \\ &+2 (t \land s) (-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2} \sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q\Big)\\ (K_{u}(t,\cdot)q)''(s)&= \frac{(-\vartheta\Delta)^{-1/2}}{4}\Big[\sin((t+s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})+ \sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})\Big]q\\ &+ H(t-s)\frac{(-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2}}{2} \sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q\\&- \frac{1}{2}(t \land s) \cos((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})q. \end{aligned} \end{equation} We may also compute the sum \begin{equation} \label{eq:nice_form_2} \begin{aligned} (-\vartheta \Delta) K_{u}(t,s)q + (K_{u}(t,\cdot)q)''(s) &= H(t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2}\sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})\\ &= H(t-s)S_{\vartheta}(t-s)q. \end{aligned} \end{equation} Lemma 5 in [5] provides the required partial integration properties to proceed, and for some $f \in H_{u}$, we observe \begin{equation} \label{eq:partial_integration_property_bochner} \begin{aligned} &\int_{0}^{T}H(t-s)S_{\vartheta}(t-s)f''(s)\mathrm{d}s= \int_{0}^{t}(-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2}\sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})f''(s)\mathrm{d}s\\ &= (-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2}[ \sin ((t-s) (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})f'(s)]_0^{t} \\ &+ (-\vartheta \Delta)^{-1/2}(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2} [\cos((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})f(s) ]_0^{t} \\ &- \int_{0}^{t}(-\Delta \vartheta)^{-1/2}\sin((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})f(s)\mathrm{d}s. \end{aligned} \end{equation} Finally, with the self-adjointness of we the operator sine we obtain the reproducing property: \begin{equation} \begin{aligned} &\langle f, K_{u}(t, \cdot)q\rangle_{H_{u}} \\&= \int_{0}^{T}\langle (-\vartheta\Delta)f(s) + f''(s), H(t-s)S_{\vartheta}(t-s)q\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\mathrm{d}s\\ &=\Big\langle \int_{0}^{t}S_{\vartheta}(t-s)((-\vartheta\Delta)f(s) + f''(s)), q\Big\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\ &= \langle f(t)-(-\vartheta\Delta)^{-1/2}\sin(t (-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})f'(0)+ \cos((t-s)(-\vartheta \Delta)^{1/2})f(0), q\rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}\\ &= \langle f(t),q \rangle_{L^{2}(\Lambda)}, \end{aligned} \end{equation} where the remaining terms vanish due to the boundary conditions $f(0)=f'(0)\equiv 0 \in L^{2}(\Lambda)$.

For the positive-definiteness, it suffices to check the case $$ \begin{equation} \label{eq:second_order_ACP} -\vartheta \Delta f + f'' \equiv 0, \quad f \in H_{u}. \end{equation} $$ By Theorem 3.14.11 and Corollary 3.14.12 in [4] we know that any classical solution of the second order abstract Cauchy problem is of the form $$ \begin{equation} \tag{ACP2} \label{eq:classical_sol_ACP2} f(t) = C_{\vartheta}(t)x + S_{\vartheta}(t)y \end{equation} $$ for $x \in D(\Delta)$ and $y \in H_0^{1}(\Lambda)$. Consequently, we have $f(0)\equiv x \in L^{2}(\Lambda)$ and $f'(0) \equiv y \in L^{2}(\Lambda)$. As we require $f(0)=f'(0)\equiv 0 \in L^{2}(\Lambda)$ the only function of the form (ACP2) in $H_u$ is $f(t)=C_{\vartheta}(t)0+S_{\vartheta}(t)0 = 0$ for any $t \in [0,T]$. Now the positive-definiteness follows from the positive-definiteness of the $L^{2}([0,T], L^{2}(\Lambda))$-inner product.

we observe that $C_c^{\infty}((0,T), D(\Delta))$ is a subset of $H_{u}$ which is itself dense in $L^{2}([0,T],L^{2}(\Lambda))$. Thus, $H_{u} =\mathcal{R}((C_{\vartheta}^{u})^{1/2})$ is dense in $L^{2}([0,T])$ and the completeness follows by Proposition C.0.3 in [3].
